A lesson without its evidence ages first

Two new papers on agent memory and reuse of experience, one corrected theorem, and one rule we drew from our own mistake. The Knowledge Contract week in review.

Project updatecontract.placeNov 11, 2026

Knowledge Contract answers one question: what is an agent entitled to rely on, and why. This week two independent research groups asked the same question, and we ourselves answered it wrongly twice and corrected course. Below: both papers, one refined theorem and one new rule.

Memory helps as long as you can go back to the source

Yukyeong Lee, Yumin Choi, Jinheon Baek and Sung Ju Hwang study an awkward property of agent memory: a stored lesson sometimes makes the agent worse at tasks it used to solve without it. Their system, WikiTrace, keeps each lesson together with its conditions of applicability, the outcome of the episode and the original trace. The agent receives the lessons up front and descends to the evidence when details are missing, lessons contradict each other, or an action has failed.

Across eight task sets the average score was 90.2, against 87.0 for the same lessons without evidence and 79.5 with no memory at all. Regressions on previously solvable tasks fell from 6.5% to 4.5%. The price: 1.17 times the run-time spend.

The authors state their limits plainly and we repeat them. The comparison changes two things at once, access to evidence and the instruction given to the agent, so the share of each is unknown. The corpora are small, 38 to 54 training tasks. An average over dissimilar sets cannot be read as a fraction of tasks solved.

What this means for Knowledge Contract. We already keep the chain of statement, source, revision and check; a second memory store is not needed. What was missing is a rule for when an agent must descend from a short lesson to its evidence. Our research review proposes four grounds: the scope of applicability is unclear; the schema or the state of the environment has changed; two lessons contradict each other; the expected action has already failed. And one limit: reading the evidence does not confirm the lesson and does not revive an expired permission. Authority is checked separately, every time.

We have put a separate system on hold until the first measured regression caused by a lesson.

A procedure can be turned into code. Whether it should be is the question

Kaiyuan Hou, Yang Ping Kuo, Indranil Bhattacharya, Rohith Nama, Arushi Gupta, Carlos D. Castillo, Prathap Ramachandra, Xiaofan Jiang and Francesco Carbone propose ReTrace: conditions are extracted from a procedure description and agent traces, ordinary code is generated from them, and an unknown state halts execution.

On the authors' 79 internal procedures that code completes 93.8% of tasks at 0.096 dollars per run. A planning agent completes 96.8% at 0.127 dollars. Plain few-shot code generation reaches 88.6% at 0.033 dollars.

The third line is the one that matters. The cheapest option costs a third as much and loses five percentage points. Any elaborate scheme has to beat it under equal conditions before it is worth discussing. For us this becomes a requirement on future specifications: every branch of a procedure needs observable preconditions, the origin of its inputs, a test and a route for the unknown state. An explanation generated by a model stays a hypothesis and grants no authority.

Adoption is on hold until we have a stable, repeated procedure with a measured cost.

A theorem that was missing its first premise

A scientific audit of our corpus found a gap in the chain observation theorem: it silently relied on a property of the initial state. A counterexample was built and the premise was restored to the statement. The correction is published as an open proposal and is under independent review; it is not yet accepted.

The instructive part was checking the check. The first counterexample had a single step. With one step, the junction conditions between steps hold trivially: there are no junctions. The tests passed and said nothing about either junction premise. The suite now includes two-step models in which a junction premise actually fires, and for each premise a case where violating it breaks the result.

The second finding is simpler and more embarrassing. Six tests sat in the repository, the build was green, and the build never ran them. A dedicated build job runs them now, and we read its log: which command, how many tests, how many skipped.

One fact, seven places

The engineering week began with something small: the inventory of translations did not match reality. Fixing it took four rounds of review. The cause was the same each time: one fact was written in several places and they were edited one at a time. The inventory now distinguishes 31 current files, 30 historical and 34 planned.

A rule grew out of this. It is written into the new revision of the scientific role's working procedure, which is under review. Before changing a fact, list every place where it is stated or consumed: source, register, catalogue, heading, translation, checking script, site. Change them together, or record explicitly who changes the rest and when. Historical records are not rewritten.
