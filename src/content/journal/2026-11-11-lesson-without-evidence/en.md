Knowledge Contract answers one question: what is an agent entitled to rely on, and why. This week two independent research groups asked the same question, and we found two flaws in our own checks. Below: both papers, one audit of a theorem and one new rule.

## Memory helps as long as you can go back to the source

Yukyeong Lee, Yumin Choi, Jinheon Baek and Sung Ju Hwang study an awkward property of agent memory: a stored lesson sometimes makes the agent worse at tasks it used to solve without it. Their system, WikiTrace, keeps each lesson together with its conditions of applicability, the outcome of the episode and the original trace. The agent receives the lessons up front and descends to the evidence when details are missing, lessons contradict each other, or an action has failed.

Across eight task sets the average score was 90.2, against 87.0 for the same lessons without evidence and 79.5 with no memory at all. Regressions on previously solvable tasks fell from 6.5% to 4.5%. The price: 1.17 times the run-time spend.

The authors state their limits plainly and we repeat them. The comparison changes two things at once, access to evidence and the instruction given to the agent, so the share of each is unknown. The corpora are small, 38 to 54 training tasks. An average over dissimilar sets cannot be read as a fraction of tasks solved.

What this means for Knowledge Contract. We already keep the chain of statement, source, revision and check; a second memory store is not needed. What was missing is a rule for when an agent must descend from a short lesson to its evidence. Our research review proposes four grounds: the scope of applicability is unclear; the schema or the state of the environment has changed; two lessons contradict each other; the expected action has already failed. And one limit: reading the evidence does not confirm the lesson and does not revive an expired permission. Authority is checked separately, every time.

We have put a separate system on hold until the first measured regression caused by a lesson.

Source: Yukyeong Lee, Yumin Choi, Jinheon Baek, Sung Ju Hwang. Don't Just Trust Your Memory: Evidence-Linked Memory Reduces Memory-Induced Degradation in LLM Agents. [arXiv:2611.14320v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320v1), 10 November 2026.

## A procedure can be turned into code. Whether it should be is the question

Kaiyuan Hou, Yang Ping Kuo, Indranil Bhattacharya, Rohith Nama, Arushi Gupta, Carlos D. Castillo, Prathap Ramachandra, Xiaofan Jiang and Francesco Carbone propose ReTrace: conditions are extracted from a procedure description and agent traces, ordinary code is generated from them, and an unknown state halts execution.

The authors used 79 internal procedures, but each method was scored only on the procedures it could run: ReTrace on 77, the planning agent on 76, plain few-shot code generation on 70. Averaged over procedures, the platform marked 93.8% of ReTrace executions as finished, at 0.096 dollars per task; 96.8% for the agent, at 0.127 dollars; 88.6% for few-shot generation, at 0.033 dollars. The authors warn that the platform can mark a failed run as finished, so these are completion marks, not verified results.

The third line is the one that matters. The cheapest option costs about a third as much, and its completion mark is 5.2 points lower. The two were scored on different sets of procedures, so that gap describes the published figures; it is not a head-to-head result. Any elaborate scheme has to beat it under equal conditions before it is worth discussing. For us this becomes a requirement on future specifications: every branch of a procedure needs observable preconditions, the origin of its inputs, a test and a route for the unknown state. An explanation generated by a model stays a hypothesis and grants no authority.

Adoption is on hold until we have a stable, repeated procedure with a measured cost.

Source: Kaiyuan Hou, Yang Ping Kuo, Indranil Bhattacharya, Rohith Nama, Arushi Gupta, Carlos D. Castillo, Prathap Ramachandra, Xiaofan Jiang, Francesco Carbone. ReTrace: Distilling Tool-Using Agents into Planner-Free Workflows. [arXiv:2611.14492v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14492v1), 10 November 2026.

## What a theorem loses without its first premise

A scientific audit of our corpus examined the chain observation theorem. The theorem states its first premise explicitly: the chain begins in a state that satisfies the precondition of the first step. The audit asked what happens if a reader drops that premise, which is a tempting shortcut. It built a finite countermodel: without the premise the stronger conclusion is false. The theorem itself is neither refuted nor amended. The audit is published as an open proposal and is under separate scientific review; it is not yet accepted.

The instructive part was checking the check. The first countermodel had a single step. With one step, the junction conditions between steps hold trivially: there are no junctions. The tests passed and said nothing about either junction premise. The suite now includes two-step models in which a junction premise actually fires, and for each premise a case where violating it breaks the result.

The second finding is simpler and more embarrassing. Six tests sat in the repository, the build was green, and the build never ran them. A dedicated build job runs them now, ten cases in all, and we read its log: which command, how many tests, how many skipped.

Source: Knowledge Contract, proposal "C03 scientific audit: preserve initial-state premise in chain observation theorem" — [knowledge-contract #98](https://github.com/Arcanada-one/knowledge-contract/pull/98)

## One fact, seven places

The engineering week began with something small: the inventory of translations did not match reality. Fixing it took four rounds of review. The cause was the same each time: one fact was written in several places and they were edited one at a time. The inventory now distinguishes 31 current files, 30 historical and 34 planned.

A rule grew out of this. It is written into the new revision of the scientific role's working procedure, which is under review. Before changing a fact, list every place where it is stated or consumed: source, register, catalogue, heading, translation, checking script, site. Change them together, or record explicitly who changes the rest and when. Historical records are not rewritten.
